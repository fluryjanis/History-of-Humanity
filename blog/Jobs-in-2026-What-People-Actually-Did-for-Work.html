Jobs in 2026: What People Actually Did for Work

A record of the work people did, the jobs they depended on, and the changing meaning of work at the beginning of the second century of the digital age.

Published September 1, 2026 · Looking at work in 2026

In 2026, work was changing rapidly. Artificial intelligence was entering offices, robots were becoming more capable, remote work had become normal for some workers, and new industries were appearing.

But most people were not living in a futuristic workplace.

Millions of people still worked in hospitals, stores, restaurants, warehouses, schools, construction sites, offices, farms, factories, government buildings, and homes.

Some jobs were changing because of computers and AI. Others had changed surprisingly little.

This article is a record of what work actually looked like in 2026 — not what people expected work to look like in the future.

Archive note This article documents the jobs and working conditions that existed in 2026.

The future of work was being debated heavily during this period. Some jobs were expected to grow, some were expected to decline, and artificial intelligence was beginning to change how many people performed their work.

Those predictions are included where relevant, but they are kept separate from the historical record of what people were actually doing in 2026.

1. Healthcare Was One of the Biggest Employers

Healthcare was one of the largest and fastest-growing parts of the American labor market in 2026.

Millions of people worked as nurses, doctors, medical assistants, home health aides, personal care aides, therapists, technicians, administrators, and other healthcare workers.

Home health and personal care aides were the single largest occupation in the United States, with roughly 4.3 million workers.

Registered nurses were another enormous occupation, with around 3.3 million workers.

Employment record U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — May 2025 Occupational Employment and Wages

Healthcare was also projected to be the fastest-growing major industry over the 2024–2034 period.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projected healthcare and social assistance employment to increase by approximately two million jobs.

Employment projections U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Industry and Occupational Employment Projections Overview

This meant that even in a period dominated by conversations about software and artificial intelligence, some of the most important jobs remained deeply physical and human.

2. Retail Was Still Everywhere

Shopping in 2026 was highly digital, but retail still required enormous numbers of people.

Retail salespersons were among the largest occupations in the United States, with approximately 3.9 million workers.

Cashiers represented another huge occupation, with around 3.1 million workers.

Employment record U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupational Employment and Wages

Stores were increasingly connected to online ordering, automated inventory systems, self-checkout machines, digital payment systems, and delivery networks.

Yet people still stocked shelves, helped customers, unloaded shipments, operated registers, managed stores, and handled the physical side of getting products into people's hands.

3. Restaurants Still Needed People

Restaurants remained another enormous source of employment.

Fast food and counter workers numbered roughly 3.9 million, while cooks represented another large occupation.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projected approximately 217,000 additional cook jobs over the 2024–2034 period.

Employment projections U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupations With the Most Job Growth

Restaurants were becoming more technological, but food still had to be prepared, customers still had to be served, and kitchens still had to operate in physical space.

4. Offices Had Become Digital Workplaces

For many office workers, the computer had become the primary tool of the job.

Documents were created digitally. Meetings could happen through video calls. Files were stored in cloud systems. Communication happened through email, messaging applications, and workplace collaboration software.

Many jobs that once required paper files and physical offices could now be performed almost entirely through a computer.

This did not mean offices disappeared.

Instead, the workplace increasingly became a mixture of people, software, digital communication, databases, and automated systems.

5. Remote Work Was Normal — But Not Universal

One of the most visible changes to working life was the normalization of working from home.

But remote work was far from universal.

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2025 American Time Use Survey, 35 percent of employed people did some or all of their work at home on days they worked, while 70 percent did some or all of their work at their workplace.

Working from home U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — American Time Use Survey, 2025

The ability to work remotely depended heavily on the occupation.

A programmer could potentially work from almost anywhere with an internet connection.

A nurse, construction worker, cook, warehouse employee, mechanic, or retail worker generally could not.

This created two very different experiences of the modern workplace.

6. The Computer Became a Workplace Tool

By 2026, computers were no longer associated only with technology jobs.

They were ordinary tools across almost every sector of the economy.

A manager could use software to track employees. A warehouse worker could use a handheld scanner. A doctor could access digital medical records. A teacher could manage assignments online. A truck driver could use navigation and logistics systems. A cashier could operate a computerized point-of-sale system.

The computer was no longer a separate category of technology. It had become part of the basic infrastructure of work.

7. AI Entered the Workplace

Artificial intelligence was one of the most discussed changes to work in 2026.

AI systems were being used for writing, research, summarization, programming, data analysis, customer support, image generation, brainstorming, and other tasks.

But AI use was not yet universal.

In a Pew Research Center survey conducted in 2024, 16 percent of U.S. workers said they used AI for at least some of their work. By September 2025, that figure had risen to 21 percent.

AI at work Pew Research Center — Workers' Use of AI in Some of Their Work

Many workers who used AI were using it as an assistant rather than allowing it to completely perform their jobs.

Common uses included researching information, editing writing, drafting reports and documents, summarizing material, generating ideas, and working with data or code.

AI therefore became another tool inside many workplaces rather than immediately replacing the workplace itself.

8. Workers Were Not All Excited About AI

The arrival of workplace AI was accompanied by uncertainty.

Many workers worried that AI could eventually reduce the need for human workers or change the value of their skills.

Pew Research found that more workers were worried than hopeful about the future impact of AI in the workplace.

In one survey, 52 percent of workers said they were worried about future AI use at work, compared with 36 percent who were hopeful.

Workers and AI Pew Research Center — Workers' Views of AI Use in the Workplace

This tension became one of the defining features of work in the mid-2020s.

AI could make certain tasks easier while simultaneously creating uncertainty about what those tasks would mean for the future of employment.

9. Software Developers Became Even More Important

Software development was already a major occupation before generative AI became widespread.

In 2026, software developers remained one of the largest sources of projected employment growth.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projected approximately 268,000 additional software developer jobs between 2024 and 2034, an increase of about 15.8 percent.

Employment projections U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupations With the Most Job Growth

This was happening at the same time that AI coding tools were becoming increasingly capable.

Rather than making software development irrelevant immediately, AI became another tool developers could use to write, inspect, explain, and modify code.

10. Data Became a Job

Modern organizations generated enormous amounts of data.

Someone had to collect it, analyze it, protect it, visualize it, and turn it into useful information.

Data scientists were among the fastest-growing occupations in the United States.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projected data scientist employment to grow by approximately 33.5 percent from 2024 to 2034.

Fast-growing occupations U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Fastest-Growing Occupations

Data had become part of nearly every major industry, from healthcare and finance to transportation, retail, entertainment, science, and government.

11. Cybersecurity Became Its Own Industry

As more of society moved online, protecting digital systems became increasingly important.

Businesses, governments, hospitals, schools, and individuals all depended on computer systems that could be attacked, disrupted, or exploited.

Information security analysts were among the fastest-growing occupations tracked by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Employment was projected to increase by approximately 28.5 percent from 2024 to 2034.

Fast-growing occupation U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Information Security Analysts

12. Trades Never Went Away

The digital economy did not eliminate physical work.

Electricians, plumbers, mechanics, technicians, welders, carpenters, machinists, maintenance workers, and other skilled tradespeople remained necessary.

Many of these jobs required physical presence and practical knowledge that could not simply be transferred into a computer.

Even highly automated societies still need people who can install, repair, maintain, construct, and physically interact with the world.

13. Construction Was Still a Human Industry

Buildings, roads, bridges, electrical systems, pipes, and other infrastructure still had to be physically constructed.

Construction increasingly involved digital planning, computer-controlled equipment, sensors, drones, and other technologies.

But construction remained fundamentally physical.

Someone still had to turn plans into buildings.

14. Transportation Connected Everything

Modern economies depended on enormous transportation networks.

Truck drivers, delivery workers, pilots, mechanics, warehouse workers, logistics managers, shipping workers, and public transportation employees moved people and goods around the world.

Digital logistics systems increasingly optimized these networks, but the physical movement of objects still required enormous amounts of human labor.

15. Warehouses Became More Technological

Online shopping created enormous demand for warehouses and distribution systems.

Workers used scanners, inventory software, automated sorting systems, robots, conveyor systems, and other technology.

Stockers and order fillers represented approximately 2.8 million workers in the United States.

Employment record U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupational Employment and Wages

Automation did not mean warehouses stopped needing people. Instead, people increasingly worked alongside machines and software.

16. Customer Service Became a Digital Job

Customer service was increasingly conducted through digital systems.

Workers could communicate with customers through phone systems, email, live chat, social media, and support platforms.

Artificial intelligence was also beginning to handle some customer interactions.

Yet human customer service representatives remained a huge occupation, with approximately 2.7 million workers in the United States.

Employment record U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupational Employment and Wages

17. Education Was Still Fundamentally Human

Schools and universities had adopted enormous amounts of technology by 2026.

Teachers used digital learning platforms, online assignments, video conferencing, electronic gradebooks, and increasingly artificial intelligence.

But teachers remained responsible for much more than delivering information.

Teaching involved explaining ideas, managing classrooms, motivating students, evaluating work, communicating with families, and responding to individual students.

Technology changed education without eliminating the need for educators.

18. Government Was Still One of the Largest Employers

Governments continued to employ millions of people in 2026.

Public employees worked in education, healthcare, administration, transportation, public safety, infrastructure, courts, environmental protection, defense, and countless other fields.

The public sector represented approximately 14.6 percent of employment in the United States.

Employment record U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupational Employment and Wages

19. Agriculture Was Still Here

Agriculture was one of humanity's oldest industries, and it remained part of the economy in 2026.

Modern farms used tractors with GPS systems, sensors, drones, automated machinery, satellite imagery, data analysis, and increasingly sophisticated agricultural technology.

But people still planted crops, raised animals, maintained equipment, managed farms, and performed physical agricultural work.

The future of farming was becoming more automated, but farming itself had not disappeared.

20. The Creator Became a Job

The internet created entirely new ways for people to earn a living from creating things for audiences.

YouTubers, streamers, podcasters, writers, artists, musicians, influencers, independent developers, and other creators could build audiences without working for a traditional media company.

A person could potentially publish a video from their bedroom and reach millions of people around the world.

This was one of the clearest examples of the internet changing not only how people worked, but what could count as work.

21. Freelancing and Independent Work

The internet also made it easier for people to work independently.

Designers, programmers, writers, consultants, photographers, editors, marketers, translators, and many other professionals could find clients without being employed by a single company.

Online marketplaces and professional networks connected workers with clients across geographic boundaries.

For some people, this created freedom and flexibility. For others, it meant unpredictable income and fewer traditional employment protections.

22. The Gig Economy

Another form of independent work involved completing individual tasks or short-term jobs through digital platforms.

Delivery drivers, rideshare drivers, task workers, freelancers, and other platform workers could receive jobs through an app rather than through a traditional employer.

The smartphone became an important connection between workers, customers, and companies.

23. Work Was Increasingly Measured by Software

Digital workplaces created another major change: work became easier to measure.

Companies could track sales, productivity, response times, completed tasks, customer interactions, deliveries, computer activity, and many other metrics.

This allowed organizations to understand their operations in unprecedented detail.

It also created new questions about workplace surveillance, privacy, productivity pressure, and how much of a person's working life should be monitored.

24. Skills Were Changing

Technology did not simply create new jobs. It also changed what employers wanted from workers.

Communication, adaptability, problem-solving, technical knowledge, customer service, and the ability to work with digital tools became increasingly important across many occupations.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics found that interpersonal and customer-service skills were especially important across several of the largest occupations.

Skills in the labor market U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — A Skills-Based Look at Occupational Data

Workers increasingly had to learn new tools throughout their careers rather than assuming that the skills they learned at the beginning of their working lives would remain unchanged.

25. The Jobs Growing Fastest Were Not Always the Jobs Adding the Most Workers

One of the interesting features of the 2026 labor market was the difference between percentage growth and total job growth.

Wind turbine service technicians were projected to be the fastest-growing occupation, increasing by approximately 49.9 percent.

Solar photovoltaic installers were projected to grow by approximately 42.1 percent.

Nurse practitioners were projected to grow by 40.1 percent, while data scientists were projected to grow by 33.5 percent.

Fastest-growing occupations U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Fastest-Growing Occupations

But some occupations with lower percentage growth were expected to add far more actual workers simply because they were already enormous occupations.

Home health and personal care aides were projected to add nearly 740,000 jobs.

Software developers were projected to add approximately 268,000.

Largest projected job growth U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Occupations With the Most Job Growth

26. Some Traditional Jobs Were Expected to Shrink

Not every occupation was expected to grow.

Office and administrative support occupations were projected to decline by approximately 3.9 percent, representing roughly 762,000 fewer jobs.

Sales occupations were projected to decline by around 2 percent.

Production occupations were also projected to decline, as were farming, fishing, and forestry occupations.

Employment projections U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Industry and Occupational Employment Projections Overview

Automation, changing consumer behavior, technology, and demographic changes all contributed to the changing structure of employment.

27. Jobs Did Not Disappear at the Same Speed Everywhere

Changes in employment were not evenly distributed.

A technology worker in a major city could experience a rapidly changing workplace while someone working in a local restaurant, hospital, farm, or construction company experienced much slower change.

Even inside the same occupation, two workers could have very different experiences depending on their employer and location.

The phrase "the future of work" therefore described many different futures happening at the same time.

28. Millions of People Still Did Jobs That Were Centuries Old

One of the easiest mistakes when looking at 2026 from the future will be assuming that technological change meant everything became futuristic.

It did not.

People still cooked food, cleaned buildings, cared for children, repaired machines, taught students, transported goods, built houses, sold products, cared for elderly people, worked farms, managed businesses, and performed countless other activities that humans had been doing for generations.

The tools changed. The basic needs often did not.

29. The Workday Itself

For full-time workers, the average workday in the 2025 American Time Use Survey was approximately 8.1 hours on days worked.

American Time Use Survey U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — American Time Use Survey

The experience of those hours depended enormously on the job.

One person could spend most of the day sitting at a computer. Another could spend the same eight hours standing in a store. Another could work outdoors. Another could move between patients in a hospital. Another could drive hundreds of miles.

There was no single 2026 workplace.

30. What Made 2026 Different?

The defining feature of work in 2026 was not that humans had stopped working.

It was that digital technology had become deeply embedded in almost every part of working life.

Computers were everywhere. Smartphones were everywhere. Internet connectivity was expected. Digital payments were normal. Cloud software was ordinary. Automation was spreading. Artificial intelligence was beginning to become a workplace tool.

Yet physical work remained essential.

Hospitals still needed nurses. Restaurants still needed cooks. Buildings still needed construction workers. Stores still needed employees. Farms still needed workers. Warehouses still needed people.

The Two Worlds of Work

Looking at employment in 2026 reveals something that can be difficult to see when focusing only on technology news.

Two worlds existed simultaneously.

One was increasingly digital. People wrote software, analyzed data, worked remotely, communicated through online platforms, created digital media, and used artificial intelligence.

The other remained physical. People built roads, cared for patients, prepared food, stocked shelves, repaired machines, transported goods, cleaned buildings, and worked with their hands.

The future of work was therefore not a simple transition from physical jobs to digital jobs.

It was the combination of both.

What People in 2026 Thought Would Happen Next

People in 2026 were already trying to understand what would happen to these jobs next.

Artificial intelligence was expected to transform many forms of knowledge work.

Robotics was expected to change manufacturing, warehouses, transportation, agriculture, and eventually many other physical industries.

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 estimated that 170 million jobs could be created globally by 2030 while 92 million could be displaced, producing a projected net gain of 78 million jobs.

Global future-of-work expectations World Economic Forum — Future of Jobs Report 2025

The same report estimated that approximately 39 percent of workers' existing skill sets could be transformed or become outdated by 2030.

These were projections, not guarantees.

But they demonstrate what people in 2026 were beginning to believe: the workplace of the future might change significantly without work itself disappearing.

Work in 2026 was not the end of human labor. It was the beginning of another transition.

A Snapshot of Work in 2026

Work in 2026 was a strange mixture of old and new.

People worked with artificial intelligence while others worked almost entirely with their hands.

Some people worked from bedrooms and home offices. Others worked in hospitals, warehouses, kitchens, farms, construction sites, stores, schools, and factories.

Some jobs were growing rapidly. Others were shrinking. Some had barely changed.

The internet had created careers that would have been difficult to imagine only a few decades earlier.

At the same time, many of the largest occupations in 2026 involved activities humans had been performing for centuries: caring for people, preparing food, selling goods, transporting things, building structures, and maintaining society.

Artificial intelligence was beginning to change the meaning of knowledge work, but it had not yet transformed every workplace.

Robots were becoming more capable, but millions of people still performed physical tasks every day.

Remote work was normal for some people, but impossible for many others.

There was no single experience of employment in 2026.

There were millions of different versions of working life.

The Historical Record

Future generations may look back at 2026 and see the beginning of a major transformation in work.

They may see artificial intelligence as the beginning of a revolution.

They may see it as an overhyped technology that changed less than expected.

They may see today's workplace as primitive.

But whatever happens next, this was what work looked like before the future had been decided.

People were still working.

They were writing code. Caring for patients. Teaching children. Driving trucks. Serving food. Building houses. Selling products. Managing companies. Farming land. Creating videos. Designing things. Cleaning buildings. Delivering packages. Repairing machines.

Technology was changing the tools people used to do these jobs.

But the work itself was still deeply human.

This Is What Work Looked Like

In 2026, the future of work had not arrived yet.

It was being built one job at a time.

Some workers were already using technologies that would have seemed impossible to earlier generations.

Others were doing work that looked almost exactly like it had decades earlier.

Both were part of the same world.

This is what we did for a living.
