What Do People Think Is the Best Form of Government in 2026?

A look at what people around the world think about democracy, government, and other ways of running a country.

Published September 2026 · Global Public Opinion Archive

Large international surveys show strong support for democracy. But is that true everywhere?

People around the world do not all have the same view of government. What someone considers a "good government" can be connected to many things, including their country's political system, economy, institutions, and recent experiences.

This article records what people thought about government in 2026. It does not try to decide which political system is objectively the best. Instead, it looks at what people said they wanted in recent international surveys and how satisfied they were with the way democracy was working in 2026.

Archive note This is a record of what people believed around 2026. Some of the international surveys used here were conducted before 2026 and are included as recent benchmarks. The 2026 Pew Research Center survey provides a newer snapshot of satisfaction with democracy in 16 high-income countries. Someone reading this many years from now should be able to see what people around this point in history wanted from their political systems, where they agreed, and where they disagreed.

What Does "Best Government" Actually Mean?

Before comparing what people prefer, we need to remember that "best" can mean different things to different people.

People may judge their political systems based on things like:

  • Freedom and Individual Rights: Being able to speak freely, practice religion, meet with others, and vote without being forced.
  • Economic Stability: Keeping prices under control, creating jobs, and helping people maintain a good standard of living.
  • Safety and Order: Protecting people from crime, violence, unrest, and outside threats.
  • Fairness and the Rule of Law: Limiting corruption and making sure people are treated fairly under the law.
  • Getting Things Done: Being able to make decisions and solve problems without too much delay or political conflict.
  • A Direct Say: Giving ordinary people a chance to vote directly on important decisions.

Because people value different things, they can support different forms of government for very different reasons.

Which Forms of Government Do People Prefer?

One major study by Pew Research Center compared five different forms of government:

  1. Representative Democracy: People elect representatives who make political decisions and laws.
  2. Direct Democracy: Citizens vote directly on major national issues instead of leaving those decisions entirely to elected representatives.
  3. Rule by Experts (Technocracy): Experts and professionals make important government decisions instead of elected officials.
  4. Strong-Leader Government: One leader has broad power and can make decisions without interference from a representative body or independent judiciary.
  5. Military Rule: The military directly controls the government.

These categories describe the systems used in the Pew survey. Real political systems can be more complicated and may contain elements of several different models.

Do Most People Prefer Democracy?

International surveys generally show strong support for democracy in many countries, although the level of support differs by country and by the way the question is asked.

In a Pew Research Center study of 24 countries, published in 2024 and based on interviews conducted in 2023, a median of 77% said representative democracy was a "very good" or "somewhat good" way to govern their country. A median of 20% said it was a bad way to govern.

The Pew study found that representative democracy had the highest median level of support among the five systems it asked about. Direct democracy was second, followed by rule by experts. Strong-leader government and military rule received lower median levels of support.

What Do Other International Surveys Show?

The Pew study is not the only international survey to find substantial support for democracy. Other surveys use different questions, countries, and methods, so their results should not be treated as if they were measuring exactly the same thing.

  • Open Society Barometer: A 2023 survey of 36,344 people across 30 countries found that 86% said it was important to live in a democracy, while 62% said democracy was preferable to other forms of government.
  • Democracy Perception Index: In its 2024 survey covering 53 countries, representing more than 75% of the world's population, 85% said it was important to have democracy in their country.
  • Afrobarometer: Across 39 African countries, an average of 66% said they preferred democracy to any other system of government.

The 2026 Reality: Supporting Democracy vs. Being Satisfied With It

People can support democracy as an ideal while still being dissatisfied with the way democracy is currently working in their country.

Pew Research Center's Spring 2026 survey looked at 19,715 people across 16 high-income countries. Across these countries, a median of 54% said they were dissatisfied with how democracy was working, while 45% said they were satisfied.

These numbers show why support for democracy and satisfaction with democracy should not be treated as the same measurement. In Sweden, Germany, and Canada, more people were satisfied than dissatisfied with how democracy was working. In the United States, France, and Greece, dissatisfaction was much more common.

Do People Agree on What Democracy Should Look Like?

Even people who support democracy do not always agree on how political decisions should be made.

Direct Democracy

In Pew's 24-country study, a median of 70% said direct democracy was a good way to govern. In this system, citizens vote directly on major national issues rather than leaving those decisions entirely to elected representatives.

Rule by Experts

In the same study, a median of 58% said rule by experts was a good way to govern. This means giving non-elected experts a larger role in making government decisions.

Support varied greatly between countries. For example, 82% in India viewed expert rule positively. Support was also high in Hungary, while it was lower in several Western European countries.

The original Pew data show that 58% in Poland and 56% in Greece viewed expert rule as a good way to govern. These figures are important because earlier versions of this article incorrectly listed 39% and 29%.

Strong-leader and military forms of government receive less support than representative democracy in the Pew comparison. However, support for these alternatives is not zero, and in some countries substantial minorities or majorities expressed support.

Rule by a Strong Leader

In Pew's 24-country study, a median of 26% said a strong leader who can make decisions without interference from a representative body or independent judiciary would be a good way to govern.

Support was much higher in some countries than others. About half or more of people in India, Indonesia, Kenya, and Mexico said this type of government could be good. Even in several high-income democracies, however, substantial minorities expressed support for the idea.

Military Government

Direct military rule was the least popular of the five systems measured in Pew's 24-country study. A median of 15% said military rule would be a good way to govern, while a median of 83% said it was a bad way to govern.

Afrobarometer found a similar distinction between rejecting military government and accepting military intervention under certain circumstances. Across 39 African countries, 66% said they preferred democracy to any other system and the same proportion rejected military rule.

However, 53% said they would accept a military takeover if elected leaders abused their power. This question measures support for military intervention under a specific circumstance, not necessarily a preference for permanent military government.

Differences Between Younger and Older People

The Open Society Barometer also found differences between age groups. Across its 30-country survey, 57% of people aged 18 to 35 said democracy was preferable to other forms of government. Among people aged 56 and older, the figure was 71%.

The same survey found that younger respondents were also more likely than older respondents to support some authoritarian alternatives. For example, 42% of respondents aged 18–35 said army rule was a good way of running a country, compared with 20% among those aged 56 and older.

Why Do Opinions Differ Around the World?

Surveys cannot always tell us exactly why one person supports one political system and another person supports something different. However, research shows several factors that are related to people's views of democracy and political institutions.

  • How Democracy Is Working: In the Pew 2024 study, people who were satisfied with the way democracy was functioning were generally more likely to describe representative democracy as a good system.
  • The Economy: Pew found that people who viewed their country's economic situation positively were more likely to support representative democracy in about half of the countries surveyed.
  • Political Representation: Across 24 countries, a median of 74% said elected officials did not care what people like them think. In 19 countries, people who said at least one political party represented their views were more likely to say representative democracy was a good system.
  • Corruption and Political Performance: Afrobarometer's analysis links weakening support for democracy to political problems such as corruption, poor-quality elections, and weak presidential accountability.

The Main Idea: Supporting Democracy Does Not Mean Loving It

One of the clearest findings across these surveys is that supporting democracy as an idea and being satisfied with how democracy works are two different things.

A person can support democracy as a form of government while also believing that their country's democracy is not working well.

The surveys show this difference clearly:

  • In Pew's 24-country study, a median of 77% said representative democracy was a good way to govern. At the same time, a median of 74% said elected officials did not care what people like them think.
  • In Pew's 2026 survey of 16 high-income countries, a median of 54% were dissatisfied with how democracy was working.
  • In the United States and France, 69% were dissatisfied in the 2026 survey. In Greece, the figure was 77%.

Being dissatisfied with how democracy operates does not automatically mean that people want to replace it with an authoritarian system. The surveys measure these attitudes separately.

When Pew asked people what could improve democracy, common themes included improving political leadership, reducing corruption, and making governments more responsive to ordinary people.

What Does the Evidence Tell Us in 2026?

Looking at the international surveys discussed in this article, several points stand out:

  1. Representative democracy has broad support: In Pew's 24-country comparison, a median of 77% said representative democracy was a good way to govern.
  2. Support does not always mean satisfaction: Pew's 2026 survey found that a median of 54% of people across 16 high-income countries were dissatisfied with how democracy was working.
  3. Views vary widely from country to country: In the 2026 Pew survey, satisfaction ranged from 76% in Sweden to 23% in Greece.
  4. Alternative systems still attract support: Strong-leader, expert, and military forms of government all received support from at least some respondents, although their levels of support varied substantially by country and survey.
  5. The questions matter: Surveys asking whether democracy is important, whether it is preferable to other systems, and whether people are satisfied with how it works are measuring different things.

There is no single form of government that everyone around the world agrees is the best. In 2026, international surveys show strong support for democracy in many countries, while satisfaction with democratic institutions and views of alternative systems vary widely.

What Will People Think in the Future?

For someone reading this archive decades from now, these numbers should be understood as a record of one particular period in history.

What people believed around 2026 does not prove which form of government is objectively the best. It also does not tell us what people in the future will believe.

People's views of political systems are connected to their experiences with political institutions, economic conditions, representation, and the way democracy functions in everyday life. Different surveys measure different parts of that experience.

This archive preserves what people said about their political systems, their expectations of government, and their views about different forms of governance around this point in history.
