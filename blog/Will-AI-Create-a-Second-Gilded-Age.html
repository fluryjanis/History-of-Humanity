Will AI Create a Second Gilded Age?

What people in 2026 thought artificial intelligence might do to wealth, work, power, and inequality.

In 2026, artificial intelligence was no longer simply a technological curiosity. It was becoming part of businesses, workplaces, education, entertainment, research, and everyday life.

At the same time, a question increasingly appeared in discussions about the future:

Could AI create another Gilded Age?

The comparison was not necessarily a prediction that the future would repeat the late nineteenth century. Instead, it came from a concern that a technology capable of producing enormous amounts of wealth could also concentrate that wealth among the people and companies that owned it.

Others imagined almost the exact opposite. They believed AI could dramatically increase productivity, lower the cost of goods and services, create new industries, and eventually make ordinary people substantially wealthier.

In 2026, neither future had arrived.

That uncertainty is what makes the debate worth preserving.

The First Gilded Age

The term Gilded Age usually refers to the period of rapid economic and industrial growth in the United States during the late nineteenth century.

Railroads expanded across the country. Factories grew larger. Industrial production accelerated. New fortunes were created on an enormous scale.

But the wealth created by industrialization was distributed extremely unevenly.

A small number of industrialists accumulated extraordinary fortunes while many workers faced low wages, dangerous working conditions, long hours, and limited economic security.

The era became associated with both extraordinary technological and economic growth and the enormous concentration of wealth and power that accompanied it.

That history gave people in 2026 a familiar comparison when thinking about artificial intelligence.

Why Compare AI to the Gilded Age?

AI was different from the technologies of the nineteenth century, but there were similarities that made the comparison appealing.

  • New technology was developing extremely quickly.
  • Huge amounts of capital were being invested into it.
  • A relatively small number of companies controlled important parts of the technology.
  • Businesses were attempting to use it to increase productivity.
  • Workers were uncertain about how automation would affect their jobs.
  • Large fortunes were being created around technology companies and infrastructure.
  • Governments were struggling to determine how the technology should be regulated.

By 2026, AI had also become a major industrial investment story. The Bank for International Settlements reported that the five largest hyperscalers were on track to spend more than $1 trillion on AI-related capital expenditure across 2025 and 2026. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The comparison therefore was not simply about chatbots or software. AI was becoming an enormous physical and economic system involving computer chips, data centers, electricity, networks, software, research laboratories, and financial investment.

The Fear of an AI Gilded Age

The central concern was relatively simple:

What happens if AI becomes extraordinarily productive, but most of the wealth it creates belongs to the people who own the AI?

If a company could use AI to perform work that previously required hundreds or thousands of employees, the company might become dramatically more productive.

If the company also owned the technology, infrastructure, data, or intellectual property that made this possible, much of the resulting economic value could potentially flow toward its owners.

This possibility became one of the major economic concerns surrounding AI.

A 2026 analysis from the Washington Post described growing evidence that AI was helping some already wealthy people and cities pull further ahead. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Academic research had also examined relationships between AI adoption, AI-related capital accumulation, and wealth inequality. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

None of this proved that AI would create a new Gilded Age. It did, however, give people a reason to ask whether the distribution of AI's benefits could become as important as the technology itself.

What Would Happen to Work?

The question of employment was at the center of the debate.

AI could make individual workers much more productive. A programmer could produce more code. A designer could create more images. A researcher could analyze more information. A small business could perform work that previously required an entire department.

But productivity and employment are not the same thing.

If one worker using AI could accomplish the work previously performed by several workers, businesses might need fewer employees.

This possibility made AI fundamentally different from a simple tool that merely helped people work faster.

In a 2026 survey summarized by Stanford's AI Index, experts were much more optimistic than the public about AI's effect on jobs. Seventy-three percent of AI experts expected AI to have a positive impact on how people did their jobs, compared with only 23% of the public. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

The gap was striking.

People building and studying AI were often more optimistic about its economic effects than the people who expected to live through those effects.

The Fear of Becoming Economically Unnecessary

Some of the most pessimistic visions went beyond unemployment.

They imagined a world in which AI could perform such a large portion of economically valuable work that human labor itself became less important.

In that scenario, simply having a job might no longer be enough to guarantee economic security.

A person could still be educated, talented, and willing to work while finding that the economy needed fewer people to perform the work they were trained to do.

This raised a much larger question:

If machines produce most of the wealth, how should that wealth be distributed?

Some people discussed stronger social safety nets. Others discussed universal basic income, wealth taxes, worker ownership, or entirely new ways of distributing economic resources.

Others argued that technological revolutions had repeatedly created new industries and jobs that were difficult to imagine beforehand.

The Other Possible Future

The Gilded Age comparison was not the only way people imagined AI's economic future.

There was also an optimistic vision.

In this version of the future, AI would become a general-purpose tool that allowed ordinary people to accomplish dramatically more.

  • Small businesses could compete with much larger companies.
  • Individuals could build products with tiny teams.
  • Scientific research could accelerate.
  • Education could become more personalized.
  • Professional services could become cheaper.
  • New industries could emerge.
  • Workers could spend less time performing repetitive tasks.
  • Productivity could increase throughout the economy.

From this perspective, AI would not create a new Gilded Age.

It could instead create an age of abundance.

The technology itself would not determine the outcome. The important question would be whether the productivity gains became broadly available.

The Question of Ownership

This was perhaps the most important question underneath the entire debate.

Who owns the machines?

In the original Gilded Age, industrial fortunes were closely connected to ownership of railroads, factories, mines, oil companies, steel production, and other productive assets.

In an AI-driven economy, the equivalent assets could look very different.

First Gilded Age Possible AI Era

This comparison was only an analogy, not a prediction.

But it revealed what many people were actually worried about: not that AI existed, but that control over an extraordinarily productive technology could become concentrated.

Was 2026 Already a New Gilded Age?

Some people in 2026 went further than asking whether AI might create a future Gilded Age.

They argued that the conditions for another Gilded Age were already appearing.

Contemporary commentary explicitly described the period as a "Second Gilded Age," while other reporting connected the enormous fortunes surrounding technology and AI with the older historical comparison. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Global billionaire wealth was also reaching extraordinary levels. UBS figures reported in 2026 showed the global billionaire population reaching a record 3,302 people, while billionaire wealth increased much faster than average personal wealth during the measured period. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

At the same time, AI was becoming an increasingly important part of the technology sector's economic expansion.

This created an unusual historical moment.

People were simultaneously watching a new technological revolution unfold and wondering whether the distribution of wealth surrounding it resembled an older period of extreme economic concentration.

What Ordinary People Thought

The debate was not limited to economists and technology executives.

Public opinion was often substantially more cautious.

A 2026 survey reported that a majority of voters believed AI would widen inequality, increasing the wealth of billionaires without necessarily increasing wealth for ordinary people. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

Another 2026 survey found that 71% of U.S. adults expected AI to reduce job availability over the following twenty years, while only 5% expected it to create more jobs. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

These beliefs are important historical evidence.

They show that the future of AI was not viewed in 2026 as a simple story of technological progress. A significant portion of the public was asking whether the benefits would actually reach them.

The Great Divide in the AI Future

By 2026, two broad visions of the future had emerged.

The Abundance Future

AI dramatically increases productivity, lowers prices, creates new industries, gives individuals powerful tools, and eventually raises living standards for most people.

The AI Gilded Age

AI dramatically increases productivity, but ownership of the technology remains concentrated. Wealth and political influence move toward the owners of AI systems while the bargaining power of ordinary workers declines.

Both futures could begin with exactly the same technological breakthrough.

The difference would come from what society did with the wealth and power that followed.

AI Would Not Decide the Outcome Alone

One of the most important observations from the 2026 debate was that technology itself did not determine how wealth was distributed.

Laws mattered.

Taxes mattered.

Labor markets mattered.

Competition policy mattered.

Education mattered.

Ownership mattered.

The choices governments, businesses, workers, and voters made around AI could influence whether its economic benefits became concentrated or widespread.

That was one reason the Gilded Age comparison was useful. It shifted the question away from simply asking what AI could do and toward asking who would benefit from what AI could do.

What People in 2026 Were Afraid Of

The concerns surrounding an AI-driven Gilded Age included:

  • Mass unemployment
  • Extreme wealth concentration
  • Declining bargaining power for workers
  • AI monopolies
  • Technology companies gaining excessive political influence
  • A shrinking middle class
  • Winner-take-all markets
  • Economic inequality between people who owned AI-related assets and those who did not
  • Unequal access to powerful AI systems
  • Entire professions being transformed faster than workers could adapt

Some people feared a world where a small group of companies controlled systems capable of performing enormous amounts of economically valuable work.

Others feared something even larger: a future in which economic power became increasingly disconnected from human labor.

What People in 2026 Hoped For

The same technology also inspired remarkably optimistic predictions.

  • Higher productivity
  • Lower costs
  • Shorter working hours
  • Higher living standards
  • New industries
  • New forms of entrepreneurship
  • Faster scientific discovery
  • Better healthcare
  • More accessible education
  • More leisure time
  • Greater individual economic power

The optimistic argument was that previous technological revolutions had repeatedly destroyed some jobs while creating entirely new forms of work and wealth.

AI could eventually do the same on an even larger scale.

Instead of creating a society divided between a tiny technological elite and everyone else, AI could potentially give individuals unprecedented economic capabilities.

The Question Nobody Could Answer

The debate ultimately came down to a question that 2026 could not yet answer:

If AI creates enormous wealth, who will own it?

The answer could determine much more than the future of artificial intelligence.

It could determine the future of work, the structure of companies, the distribution of wealth, the strength of governments, and the balance of political power.

That was why the idea of an AI Gilded Age was so compelling.

It was not really a question about robots.

It was a question about economics.

And ultimately, it was a question about society.

What a Future Reader Will Know

Someone reading this article decades or centuries from now will have an advantage that nobody in 2026 possessed.

They will know what happened.

They may know whether AI created enormous unemployment or millions of new jobs.

They may know whether AI wealth became concentrated among a small group of companies or spread throughout society.

They may know whether governments introduced new forms of taxation, welfare, regulation, or ownership.

They may know whether the phrase AI Gilded Age became a historical term or disappeared as a failed prediction.

We do not have those answers yet.

In 2026, they were still being debated.

One Day, This Debate Will Be History

The people of 2026 were living through the beginning of an enormous technological transition.

They did not know whether AI would produce abundance, disruption, inequality, or some combination of all three.

They could only look at the technology developing around them and imagine what might come next.

Some imagined an age of abundance.

Others imagined another Gilded Age.

Perhaps the most important historical fact is that, in 2026, nobody yet knew which future they were living toward.
